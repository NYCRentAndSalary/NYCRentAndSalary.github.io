Expensive Living:
The Hard Truth
about Working
and Living in NYC

New York City is known for its opportunities, diversity, and fast-paced lifestyle, but it is also one of the most expensive cities in the world. As living costs continue to rise, many people including public workers face increasing financial pressure, especially when it comes to housing. This project explores whether the salaries of New York City public employees have kept up with increasing rental costs between 2010 and 2025. Using NYC Citywide Payroll Data and the StreetEasy Rent Index, we compare salary development and rent trends to better understand how affordable it is for public workers to live in the city they help support every day.

Data: NYC Citywide Payroll Data,[1] StreetEasy Rent Index,[2] USCB American Community Survey B02001[3,4] & NYC PLanning NTAs[5] Period: 2010 – 2025 Group 35 — Sofie U. Jensen, Nikolai B. E. Johansen & Sabirin N. Omar

Introduction

A City of Contrasts

With its estimated population of 8,584,629 in 2025,[6] New York City is the most populated city in the US. The city has a large public workforce with a headcount of 306,248 in 2024,[7] with people working in fields, such as education, police, health and social services, sanitation and many more.

In 2023, approximately 63% of all housing units were occupied by renters.[8] With so many New Yorkers working in crucial areas, and providing necessary service to the city, we want to analyze whether New York City's public workers can comfortably afford to live there.

Living expenses are ever increasing — including rent — and since such a large part of homes in New York City are renter-occupied, we aim to discover whether the salaries of the public workforce has kept up.

"Can New York City's public workforce still afford to call the city home?"

In 2015, the median annual public worker salary was $68k, and the median monthly rent was $2,300. A public worker thus spent approximately 40% of their income on rent (before taxes). In 2025, the numbers have increased to $88k and $3,300, respectively, meaning approximately 45% of a public worker's salary now goes to rent - an increase of 5 percentage points over 10 years!

Section I

What Public Workers Earn

New York City's public workforce consists of many different types of jobs, in many different fields. Different jobs require different amounts of education, have different workloads and working hours, have different distributions of employees, regarding factors such as gender, ethnicity, age, and so on.

To get a picture of how public worker salaries have evolved over the years, we have picked 6 popular jobs, and analyzed their change between 2015 and 2025. This can be seen in Figure 1.

Salaries have gone up — that is surely to be expected over a decade — but how much they have gone up, differs. Furthermore, the amount alone doesn't tell the whole story; an increase of $1k per month makes a bigger difference for a person earning $5k per month, compared to someone earning $12k per month. To account for this, aside from looking at average salaries for the 6 jobs (Figure 1b), we also analyzed the average percent increase each year, compared to 2015, for each of the jobs (Figure 1a).

City medical specialists earn the most by a large margin — with a mean salary of $19k in 2025 — but interestingly enough, they are also the ones who have had the largest increase since 2015, at about 54%. Comparing that to sanitation workers, we see that they have actually received similar relative salary increases, aside from the latest one in 2025. We also see a small spike for both around 2020-2021: the time the Corona pandemic was booming globally.[9] This was a time when health was a top priority, and since many people were more at home, much more garbage was likely produced at people's homes, compared to at public places, including workplaces. These bumps thus make sense for the time period, they happened in.

"Salaries have gone up for many — but is it enough to keep up with rent and other living expenses?"

When looking at firefighters and police officers, we can see that both of these jobs have suffered from budget cuts, as their salaries fell a substantial amount after getting a large increase in 2016 and 2017, respectively. Both jobs have started receiving large mixed increases and decreases since 2021 and 2023, respectively — the data shows rather radical shifting in the prioritization of salaries in these jobs specifically, over the last couple of years.

The data indicates a significant rise in police officer salaries beginning in 2023 and peaking in 2024–2025. This trend aligns with the 2023 agreement between New York City and the NYPD's Police Benevolent Association, which ended a seven-year contract dispute.[10] The agreement granted officers multiple staged raises and an overall compounded wage increase of more than 28% between 2017 and 2025, contributing to the salary growth illustrated in the figure.

When comparing teachers to staff nurses, we see that they actually follow very similar patterns — both regarding percent increase over the years, but also the actual salary amount.

All in all, salaries have increased across many jobs, but is it enough? And do the salary increases keep up with the growing cost of renting?

Section II

The Widening Gap

At first glance, things might not seem to have changed drastically. In Figure 2, we have compared the mean salary for public workers to the mean asking rent price for 4 different apartment types, and how that has changed between 2015 and 2025 — both for New York City as a whole, as well as for each borough individually.

As stated in the Introduction section, public workers spend about 5 percentage points more of their salary on rent in 2025 compared to 2015 on average. In a decade, the median rent has increased by 42%, while the median public worker salary has increased by 28%. If this pattern continues, almost 50% of a public workers salary will be spent on rent in 2035. Taking into account that taxes have to payed, and that there are several other basic living expenses to pay, there is not much wiggle room left.

The housing situation might not be as dire, as it seems, however. The numbers in the previous paragraph are calculated under the assumption, that people live alone — this is not the case for most households. In 2023, 67% of households have more than 1 person living there. With two paychecks, the amount spent on rent falls to 27%, which is substantially more reasonable to manage. However, there are still 33% single households, and that share might be even larger for rented homes, since bigger households are more likely to earn enough to buy their own place. There are many factors to take into account to get the full picture, but the fact of the matter is, that rent has been increasing faster than salaries since 2015, and if the pattern continues, it will become less and less affordable to live in New York City on public worker salaries.

Section III

What's Left After Rent

The map in Figure 3 shows how much a public worker in each borough can expect to have left of their salary after taxes and rent, if they live and work in the same borough. For most boroughs the map indicates, that it is possible for a person to live in a studio or one-bedroom apartment on a single public income. However, the calculations don't take other expenses into account. For most of the year, the income that is left is between $1,500 and $2,500, which does not leave much for personal spending and savings.

United Way of New York City estimates that a single adult spends $624 on food, $237 on healthcare, $530 on miscellaneous, and $127 on transportation a month,[11] which leaves around $0-$1,000 in disposable income. So, while it is possible to live on a single income as a single person, it doesn't necessarily allow the financial freedom to join the housing market or put money aside for a comfortable retirement.

For two-bedroom apartments it changes a lot throughout the years, whether or not you can live comfortably on your own, and it is also very dependent on the borough. However, for three plus bedroom apartments, it would be too expensive to live on your own in most boroughs, up until 2019.

"Although it is possible to live on a single income, it leaves little to no freedom for saving money."

In 2020, rent decreased in several boroughs, making some of them more affordable. If a person was living by themselves, a studio or one-bedroom apartment would likely be enough. For a double-income household most apartments also become much more affordable, giving more financial freedom. However, for a single parent household, the circumstances are very different; while the tax calculated does not account for children in the household, and would change for a parent, the monthly expenses would also increase drastically. United Way of New York City estimates childcare costs to be between $900 and $1,600 depending on the borough and the child's age. Furthermore, health care, transportation, grocery and other expenses would increase as well. For them living for $2,500 a month would leave little to no money left, after paying the monthly bill. So, while New York City is fondly called the greatest city in the world, living there requires hard, and a lot of, work.

Across the plots, Staten Island appears to be the most affordable part of New York City, giving the inhabitants more spending money each month. Manhattan is the most expensive borough, and it is not realistic to live there on a single income. From the map we can see, that for all apartment types and most years, an inhabitant would end up paying upwards of $3,000 in rent more than what they make a month.

With the mayoral election in 2025, this all might change soon. The new mayor, Zohran Mamdani, has making New York City more affordable, as one of his top priorities. This includes free childcare for children up to 5 years old, making public transportation free, amongst other things[12]. These policy changes will hopefully help the city's residents, and give them a more comfortable lifestyle, without having to worry as much about income.

Section IV

The Disparity Between Communities

While high rents affect all residents of New York City, the communities it affects look very different. In Figure 4, we see the ten most expensive and the ten cheapest neighborhoods to live in, in New York City. Nine of the ten most expensive neighborhoods to rent in are in Manhattan and one in Brooklyn Heights — these are likely not rented by public workers, but by tenants with a high disposable income. Common for these areas are that they are mostly populated by white people, who make up between 60–85% percent of the population, compared to the 43% they represent in all of New York City. Whereas black people only make up 1–6% of the population in these areas, compared to 23% of the overall New York City population.

The ten neighborhoods with the lowest rent are in The Bronx, Queens, and Brooklyn — 7, 2 and 1 neighbourhoods, respectively. In these areas people of color make up a majority of the population, whereas white people make up less than 20% of the population in four of them, and in eight of them they make up less than the average for the entire city.

"People of color disproportionally live in areas with less public funding."

These numbers are a clear indication that there is a big disparity between race and income, and that higher income households are mostly made up of white inhabitants, whereas people of color appear to make less, and therefore live in the areas with lower rent.

While Figure 3 showed that Manhattan was mostly unrealistic to live in on a public income, one can assume that the inhabitants here work jobs that afford them a higher income, whereas The Bronx is a more likely home of a public worker. So while the other areas were more affordable, it still does not leave a large disposable income to its inhabitants. This also has other negative implications, school districts are, e.g., funded by the taxes of the inhabitants. If we look at rent prices as an indication of income, this also means that these areas have less funding for schools, and other public run institutions. From the distributions in Figure 4, this indicates that people of color disproportionally live in areas with less public funding, than the white population of the city.

Conclusion

The City They Can No Longer Afford

New York City represents opportunity and ambition, but behind that image lies a large gap between wages and the cost of living. Over the past decade, public sector salaries have risen, but not faster than the increase in rent, putting financial pressure on many workers in the public sector.

The data shows a clear trend, housing costs are consuming a large share of income, especially for single earners. While shared households can ease this burden, affordability within city limits is low.

These pressures are not evenly distributed across the city. Differences between boroughs and neighborhoods highlight the disperity between neighborhoods and the people who live in them.

Ultimately, when wages only just cover living costs, maintaining a balanced life in the city becomes hard. The question is not just whether public workers can afford New York City, but how long they can continue to do so. Hopefully this will change as the city's new administration get to work on policy changes, to make the city more affordable.
